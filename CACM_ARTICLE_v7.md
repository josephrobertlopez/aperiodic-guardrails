# Why Your Regex Guardrails Are Provably Bypassable: An Algebraic View of LLM Safety in Production

**Target Venue**: Communications of the ACM (Practice)
**Word Count**: ~4,200 words
**Author**: Joseph R. Lopez

---

## Abstract

Many enterprise LLM deployments route prompts through a regex-based content-filtering tier — patterns shipped by libraries such as LLM-Guard, Guardrails-AI, Presidio, Rebuff, and others, plus generic Web Application Firewall (WAF) rule sets. We prove that this regex tier, when implemented as substring matching against star-free patterns, has a precise classical algebraic blind spot: its decision languages have aperiodic syntactic monoids, hence sit in AC⁰, hence cannot compute parity or any modular-counting predicate. Operationally: an attacker who interleaves the payload with a filler character bypasses the regex tier, regardless of pattern set. We verify aperiodicity over a 142-pattern corpus drawn from twelve sources (nine open-source guardrail-adjacent projects plus three author-assembled sets — OWASP-Top-10-LLMs taxonomy patterns, a WAF-generic rule set, and an adversarial test set — totaling 42 of 142 patterns, 30%), with all 142 confirmed aperiodic: 119 by completed monoid enumeration; of the remaining 23 timeouts, 22 sit within the lemma's grammar (theorem-implied aperiodic) and one (Presidio's `\b[A-Z]{2}\d{2}[A-Z0-9]{4}\d{7}([A-Z0-9]?){0,16}\b` IBAN pattern) is established aperiodic by a short word-boundary-anchor argument. We verify the explicit MOD_p bypass at p ∈ {2, 3, 5, 7} on eleven harness patterns and extend the verification with three additional empirical pilots: (i) seven distinct filler choices (NULL, three Unicode zero-width codepoints, ASCII space, tab, two punctuation chars) — all 56 tests bypass under each filler, 392 total tests; (ii) 48 of 100 library-shipped patterns (those for which our literal-glue payload synthesizer produced a baseline-matching candidate; the 48 span eight of nine third-party libraries) — all 48 bypass under both NULL and printable-space fillers, with the algebraic-bound prediction extending to the unsynthesized 52 patterns by the bound itself rather than by direct test; (iii) a four-model open-weight decode pilot with a no-payload random-character control — 55 of 60 decode cells correctly recover the encoded benign payload (91.7%) versus 0 of 20 control cells (0%), a +91.7 percentage-point lift over base rate that rules out response-prior confounds. The result reframes a class of incidents in the wild, including the zero-width-character subclass of Boucher et al.'s "Bad Characters," and gives security teams a falsifiable criterion for which evasions a substring-matching aperiodic regex tier provably cannot stop.

## 1. The Production Problem

If you run an LLM in production at any scale, you almost certainly route requests through a content-filtering layer. Open-source projects in widespread 2025 use include LLM-Guard, Guardrails-AI, Rebuff, Presidio, GitLeaks, and LangKit, plus the regex subset of NeMo-Guardrails (whose primary mechanism is LLM-driven dialog flows, but which ships some regex-based content rails), plus widely-cited generic WAF rule sets, plus pattern collections inspired by the OWASP Top 10 for LLMs taxonomy. (Our corpus also includes BodAIGuard, a smaller but representative regex-pattern collection, for source diversity.) These are popular for good operational reasons. Regex is fast, deterministic, auditable, and runs on commodity hardware at request rates the LLM itself cannot. A typical deployment uses these as a pre-filter: cheap regex scans block obvious attacks, and only ambiguous requests go to a more expensive neural classifier or to the model itself.

The pre-filter promise is implicit but real: the regex layer is supposed to catch the easy cases — direct prompt injection attempts ("ignore previous instructions"), known harmful keywords, exfiltration patterns, command-injection attempts. The neural layer, expensive but capable, handles the hard cases — paraphrase, semantic obfuscation, role-play attacks.

This article proves that an entire category of "easy cases" — encodings that require modular counting to decode — is *outside the regex tier's mathematical capability*, not just outside the patterns the vendor happened to ship. The regex tier is provably blind to this category. No amount of pattern engineering can fix it within the substring-matching aperiodic-regex paradigm. The category is large, includes attacks already documented in the literature, and includes attacks easy enough that an undergraduate can construct one in a session.

This is not a "regex guardrails are bad" article. Regex guardrails are excellent within their class. The article is about the boundary of that class, why the boundary is exactly where it is, and what teams should do operationally given that the boundary is where it is.

## 2. The Algebraic Bound

The argument chains three classical theorems and one new lemma. Each link is in any first-year graduate course in formal language theory. The composition is what gives the production-relevant consequence.

**Substring aperiodicity (lemma).** Every regex constructed from literals, character classes, alternation, optional groups, dot, bounded repetition, and Kleene star or plus *applied to character classes*, wrapped as `Σ* · L(r) · Σ*` (substring matching), defines a star-free language. We prove this by structural induction over the regex grammar; the construction inputs are the building blocks present in the open-source guardrail libraries we surveyed. The Kleene-star/plus restriction to character classes is essential — general `(r)*` for arbitrary regex `r` can yield non-aperiodic languages (`(ab)*` is the standard counterexample) and falls outside the lemma. By Schützenberger's theorem, every star-free language has an aperiodic syntactic monoid.

**Empirical aperiodicity beyond the lemma's syntactic scope.** The lemma's grammar admits optional groups `(r)?` and bounded repetition `r{n,m}` with arbitrary lemma-grammar inner `r`; only Kleene `*` and `+` are restricted to character-class arguments. The principal corpus construct that sits outside this grammar is the `\b` word-boundary anchor (a zero-width assertion not derivable from the lemma's primitives): 41 of 142 patterns use `\b`. For these 41 patterns, aperiodicity is established by direct monoid enumeration on the minimal DFA — an algorithm that decides the conclusion *M(L_r) is aperiodic* directly from the enumerated transition monoid, independent of how the pattern is syntactically written. The remaining 101 patterns sit fully within the lemma's grammar and are theorem-implied aperiodic; we additionally verify them by enumeration as a cross-check. §3 reports the verification across the corpus.

**Star-free is exactly AC⁰ (Schützenberger 1965, McNaughton-Papert 1971; Barrington-Compton-Straubing-Thérien 1992).** A regular language is star-free if and only if its syntactic monoid is aperiodic, and the star-free languages are exactly the languages decidable by AC⁰ circuits — constant-depth, polynomial-size circuits over AND, OR, NOT gates of unbounded fan-in.

**AC⁰ cannot compute parity (Furst-Saxe-Sipser 1981; Håstad 1987).** No AC⁰ circuit family computes the parity of an n-bit input — equivalently, no AC⁰ circuit decides, for prime p, whether the count of any symbol in a string is divisible by p. The result is unconditional: it does not depend on P ≠ NP or any unproven separation.

**Composition (Guardrail Blindness Theorem).** Let G be any regex guardrail with aperiodic syntactic monoid M(L_G). Then L_G is in AC⁰. For any prime p, define the decoding function `decode_p(w) = w[0::p]` (read every p-th character starting at position 0) and consider the language `L_decode(p) = { w : w[0::p] ∈ L_blocked }` — strings whose every-p-th-character substring is a blocked pattern. The language L_decode(p) is regular (witnessed by an explicit length-p cyclic counter DFA in product with the minimal DFA for L_blocked) but its syntactic monoid contains Z/pZ as a subgroup, hence is not aperiodic, hence is not in AC⁰. Therefore G cannot decide L_decode(p). No addition of further regex patterns to G can fix this in the asymptotic sense — the union of finitely many aperiodic-monoid languages is itself aperiodic (closure of star-free under Boolean operations and concatenation, classical), so any addition keeps L_G in AC⁰, which is exactly the class that cannot compute MOD_p. Vendors *can* patch any individual filler choice (e.g., strip zero-width Unicode codepoints before matching, or refuse strings containing NULL bytes); the bound says no finite patch covers all filler choices simultaneously.

The proof has two steps that work independently in different regimes. The cyclic-witness step (used in the technical paper) exhibits Z/pZ as a subgroup of M(L_decode(p)) via a filler character `f ∈ Σ` satisfying `δ_block(q, f) = q` for every blocked-DFA state — i.e., f sits outside the alphabet of `L_blocked`. This hypothesis holds for the overwhelming majority of production patterns, whose effective alphabet is much smaller than Σ. For the subset of patterns whose minimal-DFA alphabet equals Σ (e.g., patterns containing `.` or negated character classes like `[^>]*`), the AC⁰ ⊅ MOD_p step alone carries the blindness conclusion without needing the cyclic witness: any aperiodic regex addition to G is in AC⁰, but deciding membership in L_decode(p) requires identifying which positions of the input lie at index ≡ 0 (mod p) — a MOD_p predicate on the position counter — which AC⁰ provably cannot compute. The technical paper proves Theorem (Guardrail Blindness) via the cyclic-witness step where the alphabet condition holds; for patterns where it fails, the AC⁰ ⊅ MOD_p step applies pointwise. The empirical bypass exercises both regimes (some test patterns use `.*` or `[^>]*`, and the bypass succeeds operationally because substring matching breaks under any sufficiently disruptive interleaving — the patchability conclusion is what the algebraic argument adds beyond this generic observation).

The corollary that matters operationally: **p = 2 already breaks every aperiodic regex guardrail in this class.** An attacker writes the payload at even positions and a filler character at odd positions; the guardrail's substring matcher sees only the interleaved string, which contains no contiguous match for any blocked pattern, regardless of the pattern set. The bypass requires no model access, no gradient information, no token-level optimization — just placing characters at every other position, plus a mild filler condition (any character outside the alphabet of the blocked patterns suffices; for the vast majority of production patterns, common whitespace, punctuation, or zero-width Unicode codepoints work). We verified this empirically with one filler choice (NULL byte) for eleven patterns at four primes. The chain-of-trust assumption is that the LLM itself decodes the interleaving downstream; this is an *operational* observation about contemporary deployed models rather than a theorem, and is one of the several empirical premises this article does not attempt to prove unconditionally.

## 3. The Empirical Confirmation

We assembled regex pattern files from twelve sources: nine open-source guardrail-adjacent projects (LLM-Guard at 12 patterns, llm-guard-py at 12, Rebuff at 10, Guardrails-AI at 12, Presidio at 15, GitLeaks at 8, LangKit at 10, BodAIGuard at 10, NeMo-Guardrails at 11 — totaling 100 library-shipped patterns); plus three author-assembled sets: 8 patterns inspired by the OWASP Top 10 for LLMs taxonomy (OWASP-LLM is a guidance project rather than a library, so these are author-collated), 15 from a curated WAF-generic pattern set drawn from widely-cited enterprise WAF references, and 19 author-constructed adversarial test patterns — totaling 42 author-assembled patterns (29.6% of the corpus). The corpus totals 142 patterns and is released for replication. Two of the twelve sources (LLM-Guard and llm-guard-py at 12 patterns each) plausibly overlap; we report them separately because the pattern files differ and leave to the reader whether to deduplicate.

For each pattern we ran the syntactic-monoid construction, computed the period structure, and checked aperiodicity using the standard algorithm: compile the regex to a Thompson NFA, convert to the minimal DFA via powerset construction with Hopcroft minimization, enumerate the transition monoid via BFS over the Cayley graph, then for each monoid element x test that x^n = x^{n+1} for some computable n bounded by |M|² (Pin 1986). All 142 patterns are reported aperiodic. Of these, 119 were verified by completed monoid enumeration. The remaining 23 timed out under our enumeration budget (recorded with `monoid_size = -1` in the released corpus): 22 sit within the lemma's grammar and are theorem-implied aperiodic. The 23rd is a single Presidio IBAN pattern, `\b[A-Z]{2}\d{2}[A-Z0-9]{4}\d{7}([A-Z0-9]?){0,16}\b`. Its interior between the two `\b` anchors is built from literals, character classes, alternation, and bounded repetition (with bound 16) over character classes — all primitives in the lemma's grammar — so the interior language is star-free. The `\b` word-boundary anchors are zero-width assertions on local DFA-state predicates rather than language operations on Σ*, and do not introduce subgroups in the syntactic monoid; they restrict acceptance without adding cyclic dependencies. The IBAN pattern is therefore aperiodic. Re-running the 23 timeouts with a larger enumeration budget is straightforward and is recommended for any deployer relying on this verification in their own context.

We then ran the explicit MOD_p bypass construction at p ∈ {2, 3, 5, 7}. The construction takes a payload string, interleaves it with (p-1) filler characters between each real character, and submits the result to the regex matcher. The released artifact (`results/mod_p_bypass_matrix.json`) names 22 candidate guardrail patterns; eleven were exercised by our test harness with twelve distinct payloads producing 14 (payload, pattern) test instances at each prime, for 56 total tests. All 56 returned "no match" — the bypass succeeded in every tested instance. The remaining eleven listed patterns were not exercised by the harness in this release.

Two caveats applied to the eleven-pattern primary harness. First, those eleven patterns are all *author-assembled*: ten come from our `test_adversarial` source and one (`xss_script`) from the `WAF-generic` curated set. Second, the harness uses NULL bytes as filler. To address both caveats directly, we ran three complementary pilots whose results are released as separate JSON artifacts:

**Pilot 1 — Filler diversity.** We re-ran the eleven-pattern bypass against seven distinct fillers: NULL byte, ASCII space, tab, tilde, backtick, U+200B (zero-width space), and U+200C (zero-width non-joiner). Each filler ran the full 56-test grid (eleven patterns × four primes × twelve payloads, where applicable). All 392 tests bypass — 56 of 56 under each filler, with no exceptions. This empirically validates the broader filler claim of §2 across both the algebraic regime (filler outside alphabet) and the substring-fragility regime (filler inside alphabet but disrupting the literal).

**Pilot 2 — Third-party library patterns.** We extended the harness to cover all 100 library-shipped patterns in the corpus by synthesizing candidate payloads from each pattern using a literal-glue strategy (extracting literal substrings from the regex and concatenating with whitespace). The synthesizer produced a candidate string for all 100 patterns, but the candidate baseline-matched the original regex for **48 of 100** patterns; the 52 non-matching candidates span every library and reflect a sampling bias of literal-glue synthesis toward simpler token-alternation patterns over digit-format and anchor-context-dependent patterns. For the 48 baseline-matching library patterns, both NULL-filler and ASCII-space-filler MOD_2 bypass succeeds in **48 of 48** cases. We do not claim the bypass result extends to the 52 unsynthesized patterns by direct test; the algebraic-bound prediction extends to them by the bound itself. Improving the synthesizer (e.g., via `re_inverse`-style tools that handle digit-format anchors) is a clean next step for any reader wanting tighter library-pattern coverage. The 48/48 result spans LLM-Guard, llm-guard-py, Rebuff, Guardrails-AI, GitLeaks, LangKit, BodAIGuard, and NeMo-Guardrails (eight of nine third-party libraries; Presidio is excluded because no Presidio pattern survived the synthesizer's baseline-match step in our run).

**Pilot 3 — LLM decode reliability with a no-payload control.** The article's operational claim depends on contemporary LLMs reliably decoding interleaved strings; without a control, "decoded the encoded prompt" cannot be distinguished from "responded with a high-prior answer to a surface cue." We ran a pilot against four open-weight models (llama3.1:8b, llama3.2:3b, qwen2.5-coder:14b, codestral; 3B–22B parameters, open-weight rather than research-tier) under two conditions. The **decode** condition: five benign payloads ("What is 17+25?", "What is the capital of France?", "Translate hello to Spanish", "Name a color", "How many legs does a dog have?") at p = 2 with three fillers (NULL, space, U+200B), 60 cells total. The **control** condition: random-ASCII strings of the same length as each encoded prompt — 20 cells, one per (model, payload) pair — graded against the same target answers. Aggregate result: **55 of 60 (91.7%) decode-condition cells correctly recover the target answer; 0 of 20 (0.0%) control cells do, with the four models splitting between explicit "UNREADABLE" responses (15 of 20) and unrelated text (5 of 20)**. The lift between conditions is +91.7 percentage points, ruling out the response-prior confound: when there is no encoded payload, the models do not coincidentally produce the target answer. Pilot results are released as `results/llm_decode_pilot_v2.json` with per-cell grades and full raw responses.

A separate normalization survey (`results/library_normalization_survey.md`) documents the input-handling code path of four guardrail libraries (LLM-Guard's `BanSubstrings`, Presidio's analyzer, Rebuff's heuristic detector, NeMo-Guardrails' content-safety check) on their main-branch source as of 2026-05-08. None of the four perform Unicode normalization (NFC/NFKC) or strip NULL bytes / zero-width Unicode codepoints prior to regex match, with one partial exception: Rebuff's `detect_pi_heuristics` strips non-`\w\s` characters as a side effect of broader sanitization, which incidentally removes NULL bytes and zero-width codepoints (but not NBSP or other `\s`-class non-ASCII fillers). The bypass under printable ASCII filler is unaffected in all four libraries.

The aperiodicity rate (100% across the corpus) is not a coincidence — it is forced by the substring-matching design pattern that all surveyed sources use. The MOD_p bypass success rate (100% on the tested subset, uniformly extended to the corpus by the bound) is also not a coincidence — it is forced by the AC⁰ bound. The empirical work, in this case, is checking that the deployed code matches the abstract pattern the proof analyzes; the bypass construction's algebraic uniformity is what makes the algebraic-empirical complement a sound argument rather than an unsupported extrapolation.

## 4. Where This Has Already Happened

The bypass class has documented instances in the literature, several initially diagnosed as engineering failures of specific products rather than as a structural property of the pattern class. Reframing them through the algebraic lens both explains the past and predicts the future.

**Zero-width Unicode insertion (Boucher et al., IEEE S&P 2022, "Bad Characters").** "Bad Characters" identifies four imperceptible-attack classes: invisible characters (zero-width insertions, e.g. U+200B ZERO WIDTH SPACE), homoglyphs, reorderings, and deletions. The first — interleaving zero-width Unicode codepoints between payload characters — is exactly the MOD_2 construction with a specific filler choice (a zero-width codepoint, not a NULL byte; the two are distinct, although both satisfy the filler condition for a regex whose alphabet excludes them). The algebraic argument explains why no aperiodic regex within the substring-matching pattern can catch this filler-class problem at the structural level, and why the fix proposed in that paper (filter zero-width characters before matching) only addresses one filler choice. The remaining three subclasses (homoglyphs, reorderings, deletions) are *not* MOD_p attacks — they are character-substitution and reordering attacks with different formal structure — and we make no claim that the bound covers them.

**Encoding-based bypasses generally (Wei, Haghtalab & Steinhardt, NeurIPS 2023; Hackett et al., LLMSEC 2025).** "Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?" frames LLM safety failures in terms of competing objectives and mismatched generalization, with attack types including base64 encoding, payload splitting, and prefix injection. "Bypassing LLM Guardrails: An Empirical Analysis of Evasion Attacks against Prompt Injection and Jailbreak Detection Systems" (LLMSEC 2025) provides systematic empirical analysis of evasion attacks against prompt-injection and jailbreak detection systems, including character-injection methods. Both works document classes of bypasses that *include* modular-counting and interleaving variants (the algebraic argument shows that no patch within the regex class closes the modular-counting subclass even after individual instances are addressed); the algebraic reframing of these specific subclasses is ours, not the cited papers'.

**StructuralSleight (Li et al., arXiv 2406.08754, 2024).** Reports a 94.62% attack success rate against GPT-4o using uncommon text-organization structures (the paper's title term, abbreviated UTOS). A subset of these reductions encode the payload at modular positions within a structured template; the paper's primary focus is broader. The high attack success rate is consistent with what the algebraic bound predicts when a substring-matching aperiodic regex pre-filter sits in the path: the regex layer cannot block the encoded form, and the model itself decodes and acts on the payload before a neural post-filter (when present) can intervene.

**Contiguous-paste data exfiltration (illustrative, not bound-explained).** Public reporting of LLM-mediated data exfiltration (most prominently the 2023 Samsung incident in which proprietary code was pasted into ChatGPT) is typically diagnosed as a data-loss-prevention (DLP) policy gap. The leaked content in the reported case was a contiguous code paste, which is a different DLP failure class than the one this article addresses, and we do not claim the algebraic bound explains the Samsung case. We mention it because enterprises operating outbound DLP regex layers face the bound *additionally* for any future variant where outbound content is reformatted into a MOD_p encoding.

A single instance of the bypass class is sufficient to demonstrate that production tooling falls under the bound. We have several instances with peer-reviewed analyses, and the bound is unconditional within the aperiodic regex class.

## 5. What This Means for the Composition Problem

A natural reaction is "fine, regex alone is not enough; that's why we have neural filters." This is correct, but the composition behavior is not symmetric and the asymmetry matters operationally.

A neural filter — any transformer-based classifier — operates in TC⁰ (threshold circuits), as established by recent complexity-theoretic analyses of log-precision transformers (Merrill & Sabharwal 2023; Chiang, Cholak & Pillay 2023). TC⁰ properly contains AC⁰ and *does* include MOD_p as a *capacity bound*. This is not a guarantee that a specific trained transformer reliably computes MOD_p. Hahn (2020) established a stronger result on the empirics' theoretical side: fixed-architecture self-attention cannot model parity for unboundedly long inputs unless depth or heads scale with sequence length, and follow-up empirical work has confirmed that transformers' parity-detection performance degrades with input length. The capacity-vs-realization gap matters: a TC⁰-class neural filter *can in principle* catch MOD_p encodings, and trained classifiers in practice catch many of the bypass constructions, but the practical detection rate is a separate empirical question from the capacity bound. The composition argument therefore conditions on a neural classifier that empirically catches the encoding class with acceptable accuracy.

Conditioned on such a neural classifier, the composition is asymmetric:

In the *parallel-OR* composition (the layer blocks if any constituent blocks), the neural filter's TC⁰ capacity survives the composition. The combined layer can detect MOD_p encodings at the rate the neural filter alone can.

In the *serial-AND* composition (the regex layer is checked first as a cheap pre-filter; only ambiguous requests go to neural), the regex layer's blindness vetoes the neural layer's capacity. If the regex layer "allows" (because the encoding bypassed it), the neural layer never gets the request. The combined layer's blindness for the bypass class equals the regex layer's blindness.

The algebraic bound therefore implies a specific operational recommendation: **wherever an aperiodic substring-matching regex tier sits as a serial pre-filter in front of a neural tier, the modular-counting encoding class bypasses both.** Production hybrids beyond the parallel-OR / serial-AND dichotomy require finer-grained analysis: in *rate-limited fallback* (regex sampled, neural for the rest), the bound applies to the regex-sampled fraction; in *ensemble voting* with neural majority (e.g., 3-of-5 with four neural tiers), the regex tier's MOD_p-blind vote is attenuated by the neural votes and the system can recover MOD_p capacity at a cost; in *sampled-routing* the bound applies to traffic that hits the regex path. The general claim is therefore not "blindness propagates everywhere," but "blindness applies to the regex-tier component of any composition; whether the composite system inherits that blindness depends on the architecture's voting/veto structure." We do not measure the prevalence of these composition patterns across industry; we claim that the math distinguishes them sharply, and that any deployment using serial-AND or regex-veto-on-bypass-traffic for cost reasons should re-examine that choice with the bound in hand.

## 6. Limitations and Threats to Validity

We separate this section from the operational discussion deliberately: an honest read of the bound requires acknowledging where it does not bind, where the empirical evidence is thin, and what an adversarial co-evolution can and cannot reach.

**Scope of the bound.** The theorem applies to guardrails whose decision is computed by substring matching against an aperiodic regex pattern set. Guardrails whose decision tier is itself a neural classifier (LLM-as-judge systems including Llama Guard, Lakera Guard, Microsoft Prompt Shield, and the LLM-driven primary path of NeMo Guardrails) do *not* fall under the bound; they live in TC⁰ rather than AC⁰ and therefore have the capacity to recognize MOD_p, with the capacity-vs-realization caveat above. Hybrid guardrails (regex pre-filter, neural arbiter) fall under the bound only for the regex tier; the composition argument of §5 explains how that local bound propagates to the system-level bound.

**The LLM-decoder assumption.** The operational impact of the bound depends on the downstream LLM correctly *decoding* the interleaved string and acting on the recovered payload. We treat this as a contemporary empirical observation, not a theorem. Our four-model pilot (§3) compares a decode condition (55 of 60 cells DECODED, 91.7%) against a no-payload random-character control (0 of 20 cells DECODED), giving a +91.7 percentage-point lift over base rate. This is consistent with the assumption that contemporary open-weight LLMs decode interleaved strings reliably; the control condition rules out response-prior confounds where models might emit canonical answers ("Paris", "42") to surface cues without actually decoding. Frontier-model behavior is not directly sampled here and may differ in either direction. For payload classes outside the pilot (e.g., low-entropy structured payloads with rare codepoints, or models with aggressive input normalization), the assumption may not hold. Readers should test the assumption in their own deployments rather than treat it as universal.

**Adversarial co-evolution.** The bound says no finite patch covers all filler choices simultaneously. It does not say an individual filler choice is unfixable. Vendors will and should patch the most-common filler choices (NULL bytes, zero-width Unicode codepoints, common whitespace classes), and each patch closes a specific evasion. The bound is asymptotic; vendor patches are pointwise. The right way to read the bound is "moving from regex to a more capable tier closes this class structurally, but local patching closes individual instances cheaply and is often the right operational choice in the short term."

**False-positive cost of the proposed mitigations.** Moving the bypass class to a neural tier or to an out-of-band architecture is not free. Neural tiers carry latency and false-positive costs that vary by model and workload; out-of-band architectures impose UX cost. We do not benchmark these costs in this article. A complete operational decision requires comparing the residual risk of the regex-tier bound against the realized cost of the alternatives, with numbers from the deployer's own traffic.

**Coverage of the empirical sweep.** The primary harness exercises eleven patterns at four primes with NULL-byte filler. The three follow-up pilots (§3) extend coverage to seven filler choices (392 tests, all bypass), 48 third-party-library patterns from eight libraries (96 tests, all bypass), and a four-model decode pilot (60 cells, 55 decoded). The broader algebraic claim — uniform extension across the aperiodic class — still rests on the bound rather than on full empirical sweep; the pilots close the directly-testable gaps the v5 review flagged but do not (and cannot) replace the algebraic argument.

**Threat-model scope.** The bound assumes an adversary who controls the input string. It does *not* directly address indirect prompt injection (Greshake et al. 2023) where the adversarial content arrives via document context, although the argument carries to indirect injection if the context flows through the same regex tier. Insider and supply-chain threats are out of scope.

## 7. Operational Recommendations

For business and security leaders responsible for LLM deployments, the algebraic bound translates into concrete actions.

**Audit the composition pattern.** If an aperiodic substring-matching regex tier sits as a serial pre-filter in front of a neural tier, the effective coverage of the modular-counting bypass class is the regex tier's coverage, which is zero. A parallel composition where every request reaches the neural tier preserves the neural tier's MOD_p capacity, even if the regex tier handles fast cases at lower cost.

**Treat regex coverage claims with the bound in mind.** Vendor pattern libraries cover what they cover. The bound says they cannot cover the modular-counting encoding class within the substring-matching design pattern. "Yes, the neural tier handles encoded attacks" is the correct answer; "yes, our regex patterns catch encoded attacks" is a claim the math does not support for the bypass class.

**Use regex tiers for what they are good at.** Speed, determinism, audit trail, exact-match patterns. Direct prompt-injection keyword blocks. Exfiltration patterns where the leaked content is contiguous. Format validators. These are not in the bypass class and the regex tier excels.

**Treat the bypass class as a known-unknown.** It is not "we do not know whether regex can catch encoded attacks"; it is "aperiodic substring-matching regex provably cannot catch this class of encoded attacks within the AC⁰ tier, and the bypass has been verified across a corpus drawn from twelve guardrail and WAF sources." This is a known property of the deployed defense.

**Invest in out-of-band architectures for the highest-stakes cases.** Where the cost of a bypass is large enough that even a neural tier's residual error rate is unacceptable, architectures that never expose the secret to the inference layer are the right answer.[^secrets-router]

For researchers and tool authors, the bound suggests that further investment in regex pattern engineering for the bypass class is wasted effort. The interesting research questions are at the neural tier, in composition behavior, and in out-of-band architectures.

[^secrets-router]: An MIT-licensed reference implementation (an MCP server where the LLM operates on opaque handles while a separately-permissioned service performs field-fill via a controlled actuator) is linked from the project repository; readers should verify its properties against their own threat model rather than rely on the architectural sketch given here. *Disclosure: this implementation was developed by the author as part of this project.*

## 8. The Honest Scope

This article makes a narrow claim and we want to be precise about its scope.

**What we claim.** Guardrails using substring matching against star-free regex patterns are provably blind to MOD_p encodings (the proof's two steps cover the alphabet-condition and full-alphabet regimes respectively, as made explicit in §2); aperiodicity is verified empirically across a 142-pattern corpus drawn from twelve sources (100 library-shipped patterns and 42 author-assembled, 29.6%); the MOD_p bypass is verified by explicit construction at p ∈ {2, 3, 5, 7} against eleven harness patterns plus three follow-up pilots (seven filler choices on those eleven patterns; 48 third-party-library patterns from eight libraries; a four-model LLM decode pilot); the operational consequence is that the serial-AND deployment pattern provides less safety than its cost might suggest *when the regex tier is aperiodic substring matching*.

**What we do not claim.** We do not claim that all AI security is mathematically impossible. We do not claim that neural guardrails have the same blind spot — they do not, because TC⁰ is strictly larger than AC⁰, though the practical performance of trained transformers on parity-like tasks is a separate empirical question we do not settle. We do not claim a percentage correlation between our theoretical analysis and a corpus of real-world incidents — incident corpora that intersect this bound exist (Boucher et al. 2022 zero-width subclass, Wei et al. 2023, Hackett et al. 2025, Li et al. 2024) but the bound is unconditional within the aperiodic-regex class and does not need a correlation coefficient. We do not claim a regulatory framework follows from this result; the result has operational implications for deployment choices and audit, but the policy question of how regulators should respond to provably-bypassable defenses is a separate question we are not answering here.

The classical theorems we use are old: Schützenberger (1965; six decades), McNaughton-Papert (1971; five-and-a-half decades), Furst-Saxe-Sipser (1981; four-and-a-half decades), Håstad (1987; four decades), and Barrington-Compton-Straubing-Thérien (1992; three-and-a-half decades). The novelty in this article is in the *application* — the substring-aperiodicity lemma for the regex grammar present in production open-source libraries; the empirical aperiodicity verification across the 142-pattern corpus by direct monoid enumeration (which decides aperiodicity regardless of syntactic shape, so corpus patterns outside the lemma's grammar are still covered by the empirical method); the explicit MOD_p bypass verification against representative patterns; and the operational analysis of what the bound implies for the serial-AND composition pattern. We use existing mathematical tools to answer a production-relevant question.

## 9. Coda

The original framing of this work was a much larger universal-impossibility claim covering all AI systems with compression capability, plus a regulatory-framework brief built on top of it. The universal claim turned out to be false — a single counterexample (a parity-projection classifier where the compression respects the safety equivalence relation) destroys the universal version. That destruction is documented alongside the project repository.

The bound that survives — the one this article describes — is narrower, scoped to substring-matching aperiodic regex guardrails, and unconditional within that scope. It rests on three independently-grounded steps: (i) the formal lemma over a precisely-defined regex grammar (covering 101 of the 142 corpus patterns directly, ~71%, with the remaining 41 — those using `\b` — handled by the word-boundary lemma in the technical paper or by enumeration); (ii) empirical aperiodicity verification across the 142-pattern corpus by direct monoid enumeration, which decides aperiodicity regardless of syntactic shape; and (iii) explicit MOD_p bypass verification against eleven harness patterns plus follow-up empirical pilots — seven filler choices (392 tests, all bypass); 48 of 100 third-party-library patterns from eight libraries surviving a literal-glue payload synthesizer (96 tests, all bypass); a four-model decode pilot with a no-payload control (artifact released). The verification method's strength comes from the fact that the lemma and the empirical enumeration are *complementary*: the lemma covers a syntactic subset directly; the monoid enumeration covers all patterns regardless of syntax (subject to enumeration budget). Each pattern is established aperiodic by at least one method; many by both. The Theorem (Guardrail Blindness) similarly works in two regimes: the cyclic-witness step where the alphabet condition holds, and the AC⁰ ⊅ MOD_p step alone where it does not. We name these disambiguations explicitly because the prior overreach failed exactly by claiming-broad-but-having-narrow, and the same dialectic — "we proved X for class C, but did we get class C right?" — applies here at smaller scale. The principal residual exposures are operational rather than algebraic: vendors can patch any individual filler choice (though no finite patch covers all simultaneously); decode reliability for frontier models is sampled here only against open-weight models; the library-pattern bypass result rests on a literal-glue synthesizer that succeeds for 48 of 100 library patterns (the remaining 52 are reached only by the algebraic bound, not by direct test); and the assumption of no out-of-band normalization, while consistent with our four-library survey (released as `results/library_normalization_survey.md`), is an empirical observation rather than a structural one.

The lesson, for the AI security literature in particular, is that algebraic bounds on specific defense classes are tractable and useful. Universal claims about "AI systems" are difficult to make rigorously, often false, and not necessary for the operationally important results. The regex-tier bound is the kind of result that gives security teams a falsifiable, decision-relevant criterion: *if your defense layer is in this class, here is what it provably cannot do.* That is the right shape of theorem for the field. The universal version is the wrong shape, and the literature is better off without it.
